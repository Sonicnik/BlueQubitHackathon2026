What does one predicted number mean?

Your first skill: turn measurement probabilities into a signed signal, and interpret the sign. This is the foundation for reading the challenge's per-qubit outputs; its precise echo construction comes later.

Begin with the measurement

A qubit has two standard measurement outcomes: 0 and 1. A quantum state supplies an amplitude for each; the squared magnitude gives that outcome's probability. We will unpack amplitudes next. IBM: measurement rule.

For now, imagine preparing the same state repeatedly and measuring it. Each run gives one bit. The probability describes the distribution across repeated preparations.

Assign scores to those outcomes

For the observable called Pauli Z, outcome 0 has score +1 and outcome 1 has score −1. Its matrix is diagonal with entries +1 and −1. IBM: Pauli matrices.

The expectation value is the probability-weighted average of these scores:

⟨Z⟩ = (+1) P(0) + (−1) P(1)
= P(0) − P(1)

Example: if P(0) = 0.65 and P(1) = 0.35, the expectation is 0.30. In 100 outcomes with exactly 65 zeros and 35 ones, the observed average would also be (65 − 35) / 100 = 0.30. Random samples need not have exactly these counts.

The number 0.30 is an average score. Each individual Z measurement still gives +1 or −1.

Explore the sign

Blue: outcome 0. Orange: outcome 1. These are probabilities, not a simulation of individual shots.

Before moving the slider: predict the signal when zeros and ones are equally likely. Then check. A negative expectation means outcome 1 is more likely; probabilities themselves remain nonnegative.
